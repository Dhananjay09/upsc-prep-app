MINERAL BELTS OF INDIA (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong)
India's minerals concentrate in three broad belts: (i) the North-Eastern Plateau Region (Chotanagpur/Jharkhand, Orissa Plateau, West Bengal, parts of Chhattisgarh) — richest and most diversified, with iron ore, coal, manganese, bauxite, mica; (ii) the South-Western Plateau Region (Karnataka, Goa, Tamil Nadu uplands, Kerala) — rich in ferrous metals and bauxite, high-grade iron ore, manganese, limestone, but lacks coal (except Neyveli lignite); (iii) the North-Western Region (Aravali belt, Rajasthan and Gujarat, associated with Dharwar rocks) — copper, zinc, building stones (sandstone, granite, marble), gypsum, Fuller's earth, and Gujarat's petroleum and salt deposits. A separate Himalayan belt has copper, lead, zinc, cobalt and tungsten, plus Assam valley's mineral oil.

IRON ORE AND MANGANESE (India-specific detail)
India has the largest iron ore reserves in Asia (about 20 billion tonnes, 2004-05), of two main types — haematite and magnetite — with about 95% located in Orissa, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka, Goa, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu; a key advantage is that iron ore mines lie close to coalfields in the north-eastern plateau. Manganese, essential for smelting iron ore, is led by Orissa, followed by Karnataka, Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh — though Maharashtra's manganese mines suffer from being located far from steel plants.

COAL IN INDIA (complementing, not duplicating, Goh Cheng Leong's world coal survey)
About 80% of India's coal is bituminous, non-coking grade. Coal occurs in rocks of two geological ages: Gondwana (the dominant, older type) and Tertiary. The most important Gondwana coalfields lie in the Damodar Valley (Jharkhand-Bengal coal belt) — Raniganj, Jharia, Bokaro, Giridih, Karanpura — with Jharia the largest field, followed by Raniganj. Tertiary coals occur in Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya and Nagaland. Lignite (brown coal) occurs in coastal Tamil Nadu, Pondicherry, Gujarat and Jammu & Kashmir (Neyveli being the notable lignite field) — distinct from Goh Cheng Leong's account of world lignite being concentrated in Germany and the U.S.S.R.'s Tula deposits.

PETROLEUM AND NATURAL GAS IN INDIA (India-specific detail)
Crude petroleum in India occurs in sedimentary rocks of the tertiary period. Digboi (Assam) was India's only oil-producing region until the Oil and Natural Gas Commission (ONGC) was set up in 1956, after which Ankaleshwar, Kalol, Mehsana and other Gujarat fields, and Mumbai High (discovered 1973, 160 km off Mumbai, production from 1976) were developed; newer finds occur in the Krishna-Godavari and Kaveri basins on the east coast. India has two refinery types: field-based (e.g., Digboi) and market-based (e.g., Barauni). The Gas Authority of India Limited (GAIL), set up in 1984, transports and markets natural gas, with reserves along the eastern coast (Tamil Nadu, Orissa, Andhra Pradesh), Tripura, Rajasthan, and offshore Gujarat/Maharashtra.

NUCLEAR ENERGY IN INDIA (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong)
India's Atomic Energy Commission was established in 1948; its Atomic Energy Institute at Trombay (1954) was renamed the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre in 1967. Uranium deposits occur along the Singhbhum copper belt and in Rajasthan, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra and Himachal Pradesh; thorium comes from monazite/ilmenite beach sands of Kerala and Tamil Nadu, and near Vishakhapatnam and the Mahanadi delta. Major nuclear power projects include Tarapur (Maharashtra), Rawatbhata near Kota (Rajasthan), Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu), Narora (Uttar Pradesh), Kaiga (Karnataka) and Kakarapara (Gujarat).

NON-CONVENTIONAL ENERGY SOURCES IN INDIA (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong, who covers only conventional coal/oil/hydro/thermal power)
NCERT classifies solar, wind, tidal/wave, geothermal and bio-energy as renewable, more equitably distributed and environment-friendly alternatives to exhaustible fossil fuels. Solar energy (via photovoltaic cells or solar thermal technology) is claimed to be about 7% more effective than coal/oil plants and 10% more effective than nuclear plants; western India (Gujarat, Rajasthan) has the greatest solar potential. Wind energy: India's potential exceeds 50,000 MW; the largest wind farm in Asia is at Lamba in Kachchh, Gujarat, with another major plant at Tuticorin, Tamil Nadu. Tidal/wave energy potential exists along India's west coast but remains largely undeveloped. Geothermal energy is being tapped at Manikaran, Himachal Pradesh (the first successful geothermal tapping worldwide was at Boise, Idaho, USA, in 1890). Bio-energy converts agricultural/municipal/industrial waste into electricity, heat or cooking gas — e.g., the Okhla (Delhi) municipal-waste-to-energy project.

CONSERVATION OF MINERAL RESOURCES (India-specific policy framing, new content)
NCERT frames mineral conservation around sustainable development: replacing exhaustible resources with inexhaustible alternatives (solar, wind, wave, geothermal), recycling scrap metals (especially copper, lead, zinc, where India's own reserves are meagre), using substitutes for scarce metals, and reducing the export of strategic/scarce minerals to preserve reserves for longer domestic use.
