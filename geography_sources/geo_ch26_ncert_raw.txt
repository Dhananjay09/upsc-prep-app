LAND-USE CATEGORIES (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong)
Land-use records maintained by India's Land Revenue Department classify land into nine categories: (i) Forests — area demarcated by government for forest growth, which may differ from actual forest cover; (ii) Land put to Non-agricultural Uses — settlements, infrastructure, industries; (iii) Barren and Wastelands — hilly terrain, deserts, ravines, not cultivable with available technology; (iv) Area under Permanent Pastures and Grazing Lands — mostly owned by village panchayats/government, a Common Property Resource; (v) Area under Miscellaneous Tree Crops and Groves — mostly privately owned orchards; (vi) Culturable Wasteland — land fallow for more than five years, reclaimable; (vii) Current Fallow — land uncultivated for one year or less; (viii) Fallow other than Current Fallow — uncultivated for more than one but less than five years; (ix) Net Area Sown — physical extent of land on which crops are sown and harvested.

Common Property Resources (CPRs) are community-owned (not privately owned) resources — such as community forests, pasture lands, and village water bodies — where every member has a right of use without individual property rights; especially important for landless/marginal farmers and for women (who do much of the fodder/fuel collection).

CROPPING SEASONS IN INDIA (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong, who does not use these terms)
Three distinct crop seasons exist in northern/interior India: Kharif (coincides with the South-West Monsoon; crops include rice, cotton, jute, jowar, bajra, tur); Rabi (begins with onset of winter, October-November, ends March-April; low temperatures suit temperate/subtropical crops like wheat, gram, mustard); Zaid (a short summer season after rabi harvest, for watermelons, cucumbers, vegetables, fodder crops on irrigated land). This three-season distinction does not exist in southern India, where high year-round temperatures allow the same tropical crops to be grown up to three times a year given sufficient soil moisture.

TYPES OF FARMING BY MOISTURE SOURCE (new content)
Farming is classified as irrigated or rainfed (barani). Irrigated farming may be protective (supplementing rainfall to protect crops from moisture deficiency, spread over maximum area) or productive (providing high water input per unit area for high productivity). Rainfed farming is further split into dryland farming (regions with less than 75 cm annual rainfall, growing hardy/drought-resistant crops like ragi, bajra, moong, gram, guar) and wetland farming (rainfall exceeds soil moisture needs; grows water-intensive crops like rice, jute, sugarcane; may face flood/erosion hazards).

INDIA-SPECIFIC CROP DETAILS (complementing, not duplicating, Goh Cheng Leong's own world-scale rice/wheat sections)
Rice: staple food for most of India's population; about 3,000 varieties grown from sea level to about 2,000 m altitude; West Bengal farmers grow three rice crops in a year called 'aus', 'aman' and 'boro'; India contributes about 22% of world rice production, ranking second after China; top rice states (2002-03): West Bengal, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu; rice cultivation in irrigated Punjab/Haryana was introduced only in the 1970s following the Green Revolution, despite these not being traditionally rice-growing areas.
Wheat: India's second most important cereal after rice, contributing about 12% of world production; primarily a rabi (winter) crop; about 85% of area is in the Indo-Gangetic Plain, Malwa Plateau and Himalayas (up to 2,700 m); top states: Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh.
Pulses: India is a leading world producer, accounting for about one-fifth of world pulse production; mainly rainfed crops of the Deccan/central plateau drylands; gram and tur are the main pulses.

THE GREEN REVOLUTION (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong, a specific post-Independence Indian agricultural policy episode)
After Independence, India's initial strategy to boost foodgrain output was: (i) switching from cash crops to food crops, (ii) intensifying cropping on existing land, (iii) bringing more fallow/cultivable land under the plough. This helped initially, but production stagnated in the late 1950s, and two consecutive droughts in the mid-1960s caused a food crisis requiring foodgrain imports. By the mid-1960s, new High-Yielding Varieties (HYVs) of wheat (from Mexico) and rice (from the Philippines) became available. India introduced a package technology of HYVs plus chemical fertilisers in irrigated areas of Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh and Gujarat — assured irrigation being a pre-requisite for success. This rapid growth spurt became known as the 'Green Revolution', making India self-reliant in foodgrain production, though initially confined to irrigated areas only, causing regional disparities until it spread to Eastern/Central India after the 1970s. In 1988 the Planning Commission initiated agro-climatic planning to promote regionally balanced development, also emphasising diversification into dairy, poultry, horticulture, livestock and aquaculture.

PROBLEMS OF INDIAN AGRICULTURE (new content, complementary to but distinct from Goh Cheng Leong's general 'other factors' discussion)
Despite Green Revolution gains, Indian crop yields (rice, wheat, cotton, oilseeds) remain much lower than in the U.S.A., Russia and Japan; labour productivity is also low due to high population pressure on land. Financial constraints and indebtedness are severe, as modern agricultural inputs are expensive and many marginal/small farmers have little or no savings, forcing reliance on credit and sometimes leading to farmer distress. Historically, unequal land distribution under British-era revenue systems (Mahalwari, Ryotwari, and the especially exploitative Zamindari system) left a legacy that post-Independence land reforms only partially addressed.
