FOUR TYPES OF RURAL SETTLEMENT IN INDIA (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong's simple dispersed/nucleated dichotomy)
NCERT classifies Indian rural settlements into four types, driven by physical (terrain, altitude, climate, water), cultural/ethnic (social structure, caste, religion) and security factors: (i) Clustered/agglomerated/nucleated — compact, distinct from surrounding farms, common in fertile alluvial plains and the north-east, but also for defence (Bundelkhand, Nagaland) or water-scarcity reasons (Rajasthan); (ii) Semi-clustered/fragmented — clustering in a restricted area or fragmentation of a large village, often along caste lines (dominant community central, lower-strata/menial workers on the outer flanks), widespread in the Gujarat plain and parts of Rajasthan; (iii) Hamleted — fragmented into physically separated units bearing a common name (locally called panna, para, palli, nagla, dhani), common in the middle/lower Ganga plain, Chhattisgarh and lower Himalayan valleys; (iv) Dispersed/isolated — isolated huts/hamlets in remote jungles or hill slopes, found in Meghalaya, Uttaranchal, Himachal Pradesh and Kerala.

EVOLUTION OF INDIAN TOWNS (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong's site/situation-based world examples)
Towns in India date to the Indus Valley Civilization (Harappa, Mohenjodaro). NCERT classifies Indian towns by era: Ancient towns (2,000+ years old, mostly religious/cultural centres, e.g. Varanasi, Prayag/Allahabad, Pataliputra/Patna, Madurai); Medieval towns (about 100 existing towns, mostly fort-towns and headquarters of principalities built on ancient-town ruins, e.g. Delhi, Hyderabad, Jaipur, Lucknow, Agra, Nagpur); Modern towns (developed by the British and other Europeans, starting as coastal trading ports — Surat, Daman, Goa, Pondicherry — before the British consolidated power around Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata; post-1850 industrial towns like Jamshedpur also emerged). After Independence, new administrative-headquarters towns (Chandigarh, Bhubaneswar, Gandhinagar, Dispur) and industrial centres (Durgapur, Bhilai, Sindri, Barauni) were developed, alongside satellite towns around metros (Ghaziabad, Rohtak, Gurgaon around Delhi).

URBANISATION TRENDS AND TOWN-SIZE CLASSIFICATION IN INDIA (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong)
India's urbanisation level (percentage of urban to total population) was only 28% in 2001, low compared to developed countries, even though total urban population grew elevenfold over the 20th century; the urbanisation growth rate has slowed in the last two decades (per the source's writing). The Census of India classifies urban centres into six size-based classes; a Class I town/city has over 1 lakh (100,000) population, a metropolitan city has 1-5 million, and a mega city exceeds 5 million. In 2001, more than 60% of India's urban population lived in Class I towns; of 423 such cities, 35 were metropolitan cities, including 6 mega cities (Greater Mumbai — the largest agglomeration at 16.4 million — along with Kolkata, Delhi, Chennai, Bangalore and Hyderabad), together housing over one-fifth (21%) of India's urban population.

FUNCTIONAL CLASSIFICATION OF INDIAN TOWNS (complementing, with India-specific examples, Goh Cheng Leong's world functional-town classification)
NCERT's functional categories for India include: administrative towns (Chandigarh, New Delhi, Bhopal, Shillong, Guwahati, Imphal, Srinagar, Gandhinagar); industrial towns (Mumbai, Salem, Coimbatore, Modinagar, Jamshedpur, Hugli, Bhilai); transport cities, split into ports (Kandla, Kochchi, Kozhikode, Vishakhapatnam) and inland transport hubs (Agra, Dhulia, Mughal Sarai, Itarsi, Katni); commercial towns (Kolkata, Saharanpur, Satna); mining towns (Raniganj, Jharia, Digboi, Ankaleshwar, Singrauli); and garrison/cantonment towns (Ambala, Jalandhar, Mhow, Babina, Udhampur).
