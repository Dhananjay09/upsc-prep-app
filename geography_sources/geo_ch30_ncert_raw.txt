INDIA'S ROAD NETWORK AND HIGHWAYS (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong's world-scale treatment)
India (2005) has one of the world's largest road networks, 33.1 lakh km, carrying about 85% of passenger and 70% of freight traffic. The historic Grand Trunk (GT) Road, originally the Shahi Road built by Sher Shah Suri connecting the Indus Valley to the Sonar Valley in Bengal, was renamed during British rule and today survives as National Highway-1 (Delhi-Amritsar) and NH-2 (Delhi-Kolkata). Roads are classified as National Highways (2% of total road length but carrying 40% of road traffic), State Highways (4%), Major District Roads (14%) and Rural Roads (80% — vital for rural connectivity, though density is lowest in hilly/forested/plateau terrain). The National Highways Authority of India (NHAI), operationalised in 1995, executes major projects including the Golden Quadrilateral (5,846 km, 4/6-lane corridor linking Delhi-Mumbai-Chennai-Kolkata) and the North-South Corridor (Srinagar to Kanniyakumari, 4,076 km) / East-West Corridor (Silchar to Porbandar, 3,640 km). The Border Roads Organisation (BRO), established 1960, builds/maintains strategic roads in the Himalayan border regions (e.g., the Chandigarh-Manali-Leh road, averaging 4,270 m altitude) and had completed over 40,450 km of roads by 2005. Road density (length per 100 sq km) varies from just 10.48 km in Jammu & Kashmir to 387.24 km in Kerala, against a national average of 75.42 km — driven mainly by terrain and economic development level.

INDIAN RAILWAYS (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong's Trans-Siberian/Canadian Pacific focus)
India's first railway line ran from Bombay to Thane (34 km) in 1853. Indian Railways, the country's largest government undertaking, has a network of 63,221 km, divided into sixteen zones for management given its enormous scale. Three gauge categories exist: broad gauge (1.676 m, 74.14% of route length), metre gauge (1 m, 21.02%), and narrow gauge (0.762 m or 0.610 m, confined mostly to hilly areas, 4.94%) — India has since pursued a 'uni-gauge' policy converting metre/narrow gauge lines to broad gauge, alongside replacing steam engines with diesel/electric traction. The Konkan Railway (completed 1998), an 'engineering marvel' 760 km long connecting Roha (Maharashtra) to Mangalore (Karnataka), crosses 146 rivers/streams, nearly 2,000 bridges and 91 tunnels, including Asia's then-largest railway tunnel (about 6.5 km) — built as a joint undertaking of Maharashtra, Goa and Karnataka. Railway density remains lowest in the hill states, north-eastern states, central India and Rajasthan.

INLAND WATERWAYS IN INDIA (complementing Goh Cheng Leong's general treatment of inland waterways)
NCERT classifies Indian water transport into inland waterways and oceanic waterways. Inland waterways were India's chief transport mode before railways arrived, but have since faced tough competition from road and rail, compounded by the diversion of river water for irrigation reducing navigable depth — a specifically Indian constraint not discussed in Goh Cheng Leong's account of European/North American inland waterways.
