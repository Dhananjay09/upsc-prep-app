INDIA'S POPULATION DISTRIBUTION AND DENSITY (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong's world-scale treatment)
India's first population census was conducted in 1872, but the first complete census only in 1881; censuses are now held every ten years. India (2001) had 1,028 million people, the second most populous country after China, with population larger than North America, South America and Australia combined. Population distribution is highly uneven: Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Bihar, West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh (plus Tamil Nadu, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Karnataka, Gujarat) together account for about 76% of India's population, while states like Arunachal Pradesh (0.11%) have a very small population share despite large area. India's population density (2001) was 313 persons per sq km, ranking third in Asia after Bangladesh (849) and Japan (334); density rose from 117/sq km in 1951 to 313/sq km in 2001. Density ranges from just 13 persons/sq km in Arunachal Pradesh to 9,340 persons/sq km in the National Capital Territory of Delhi.

PHYSIOLOGICAL AND AGRICULTURAL DENSITY (new terminology, entirely absent from Goh Cheng Leong)
Physiological density = total population / net cultivated area. Agricultural density = total agricultural population (cultivators, agricultural labourers and their families) / net cultivable area. These offer a better human-land pressure measure than simple crude density, especially relevant for an agriculture-dependent population like India's.

FOUR PHASES OF INDIA'S POPULATION GROWTH, 1901-2001 (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong, who treats only global growth trends)
Phase I (1901-1921): the 'stagnant/stationary phase' — both birth and death rates were high, with growth even turning negative in 1911-1921, due to poor health/medical services, illiteracy and inefficient food distribution.
Phase II (1921-1951): 'steady population growth' — improved health/sanitation lowered the mortality rate while birth rate stayed high, despite the Great Depression and World War II.
Phase III (1951-1981): the 'population explosion' phase — rapid mortality decline combined with continued high fertility produced an average annual growth rate of 2.2%; post-Independence planning improved living conditions, and international migration (Tibetans, Bangladeshis, Nepalis, Pakistanis) added to growth.
Phase IV (post-1981 to present): growth remains high but is gradually slowing, driven by a declining crude birth rate linked to a rising mean age at marriage and improved female education. India's population was projected (World Development Report) to reach 1,350 million by 2025; the annual growth rate as of the chapter's writing was 2.4%, implying a population-doubling time of about 36 years — at which pace India was expected to surpass China's population.

REGIONAL VARIATION IN INDIA'S POPULATION GROWTH (India-specific pattern)
Southern/eastern states — Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Orissa, Pondicherry, Goa — showed low 1991-2001 decadal growth (under 20%), with Kerala recording India's lowest growth rate (9.4%). A continuous north-west-to-north-central belt (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Uttaranchal, Madhya Pradesh, Sikkim, Assam, West Bengal, Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand) averaged a higher 20-25% decadal growth rate.

ADOLESCENT POPULATION AND YOUTH POLICY (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong)
Adolescents (age 10-19) made up about 22% of India's population (2001), 53% male and 47% female. NCERT flags associated challenges: low age at marriage, female illiteracy, school dropouts, poor nutrition, high adolescent maternal mortality, HIV/AIDS risk, and juvenile delinquency. The National Youth Policy (launched 2003) was designed to address the overall development of India's youth and adolescent population.
