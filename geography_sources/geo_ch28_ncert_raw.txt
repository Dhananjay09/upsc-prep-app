INDIA'S INTEGRATED IRON AND STEEL PLANTS (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong, who covers only world/Jamshedpur-level detail)
TISCO (Tata Iron and Steel Company), India's oldest private-sector integrated plant, lies near the Mumbai-Kolkata railway line, about 240 km from Kolkata (its nearest export port); iron ore comes from Noamundi and Badam Pahar, coking coal from Jharia and West Bokaro, and water from the Subarnarekha and Kharkai rivers. IISCO (Indian Iron and Steel Company) operates three plants (Hirapur, Kulti, Burnpur) near the Damodar Valley coalfields (Raniganj, Jharia, Ramgarh), using Singhbhum iron ore; it was nationalised after production fell in 1972-73. The Visvesvaraiya Iron and Steel Works (VISL), near Kemangundi in the Bababudan Hills, Karnataka, has local iron ore, limestone and manganese but NO local coal — it used charcoal until 1951, then switched to electric furnaces powered by Jog Falls hydroelectricity, and produces specialised steels/alloys.
During the Second Five Year Plan (1956-61), three public-sector plants were built with foreign collaboration under Hindustan Steel Limited (later folded into the Steel Authority of India Limited, SAIL, in 1973): Rourkela (Orissa, with Germany; power from the Hirakud project), Bhilai (Chhattisgarh, with the U.S.S.R.; iron ore from Dalli-Rajhara, coal from Korba/Kargali, power from Korba Thermal), and Durgapur (West Bengal, with the U.K.; coal from Raniganj/Jharia, iron ore from Noamundi, power from the Damodar Valley Corporation). Bokaro (1964, Russian collaboration) forms a 'Bokaro-Rourkela combine', with wagons carrying iron ore one way and coal the other. Later plants — Vizag (Vishakhapatnam, 1992, India's first port-based plant), Vijaynagar (Hospet, Karnataka, indigenous technology) and Salem (Tamil Nadu, 1982) — were deliberately sited in South India, away from the traditional raw-material crescent (Chhattisgarh-Orissa-Jharkhand-West Bengal), reflecting a shift toward market/port-based location.

THE COTTON TEXTILE INDUSTRY IN INDIA (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong's world-scale textile treatment)
India's first modern cotton mill was established in Mumbai in 1854; Mumbai's advantages included proximity to Gujarat/Maharashtra cotton, port facilities, capital availability (as India's financial centre), and abundant labour. Because cotton is a 'pure' (non-weight-losing) raw material, unlike iron ore, its location is not tied to the raw-material source — later mills spread based on market, cheap labour, or hydel power availability (e.g., Tamil Nadu's rapid growth was driven by abundant hydel power, not local cotton). By 1947 India had 423 mills, but partition transferred most good cotton-growing land to West Pakistan, leaving India with only 29% of the cotton-producing area. The industry today splits into an organised (mill) sector, which has shrunk from 81% of production in the mid-20th century to about 6% by 2000, and a decentralised sector (powerlooms ~59%, handlooms ~19% of output) that now dominates. Tamil Nadu has India's largest number of mills (mostly yarn-spinning, centred at Coimbatore); other major states are Maharashtra, Gujarat, West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh.

THE SUGAR INDUSTRY IN INDIA (entirely new content vs Goh Cheng Leong)
India is the world's largest producer of both sugarcane and cane sugar, contributing about 8% of world sugar production, alongside khandsari and gur/jaggery also made from sugarcane. Modern sugar milling began in Bihar in 1903, later spreading to Uttar Pradesh. Since sugarcane is a weight-losing crop whose sucrose content degrades rapidly after harvest (best recovery requires crushing within 24 hours), sugar factories must be located very close to cane-growing fields — a much tighter raw-material constraint than for cotton textiles.
